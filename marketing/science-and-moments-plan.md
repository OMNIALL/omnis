## 0. Decision: no doctor photos, names-as-endorsers or attributed quotes

The proposed "شنوا يقولوا خبراء و علماء الأعصاب" block (photos of Andrew Huberman and John J. Foxe with quotes)
**should not be published**:

| Problem | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Implied endorsement** — their faces + names next to a product page say "these experts recommend OMNIS". Neither has endorsed OMNIS. | Using a real person's image/name to sell without consent = legal exposure (image rights, false endorsement / misleading advertising). |
| **Attributed quotes they didn't say** — the Arabic texts are paraphrases put in their mouths. | Misrepresentation; easy to debunk; destroys trust. |
| **Copyrighted photos** | Copyright claims, takedowns. |
| **Ad platforms** | Meta/TikTok reject ads using public figures without authorization (and can restrict the ad account). |
| **Claims** — "reduces stress" (التوتر) | Outside the claims rulebook (`CLAUDE.md`). |

**The compliant way to get the same credibility:** cite the **published research itself** — authors, journal, year,
link — clearly labelled as research on the *ingredients*, not on OMNIS. That is factual citation, not endorsement.
Bonus: John J. Foxe **is an author** of two of these studies, so his work is cited accurately, without his face or
invented words.

### Wording rules for this section
- Describe **what the study measured** ("précision sur une tâche de changement d'attention"), never generalize to
  "améliore la concentration de tout le monde".
- No "prouvé", "cliniquement prouvé", "garantit". Use « dans cette étude », « par rapport au placebo ».
- Never put the studies' results in the hero or in ad headlines as product claims. Ads may say
  « Caféine + L-théanine : un duo étudié depuis 2008 » + link to `/science`.
- Before running ads that reference studies: validate the wording with a Tunisian pharmacist / regulatory advisor.
